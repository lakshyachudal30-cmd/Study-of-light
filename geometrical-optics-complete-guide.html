01 / Foundations

What is geometrical optics?

Geometrical optics (ray optics) models light as straight-line rays. It is especially useful when objects and apertures are much larger than the wavelength of light and diffraction can be neglected.

Definition

Light ray

An ideal line showing the direction in which light energy travels. A bundle of rays forms a beam. Rays are a model, not thin physical wires.

Principle

Rectilinear propagation

In a homogeneous, transparent medium, light travels along straight paths. This explains sharp shadows and the operation of a pinhole camera.

Principle

Independence of rays

When light beams cross, they continue along their own paths; under ordinary linear conditions, the beams do not disturb one another.

Principle

Reversibility

If a light ray travels from A to B along a particular optical path, it can travel from B to A along the same path in reverse.

Wavefronts and rays

A wavefront is a surface joining points of a wave that are in the same phase. A ray is perpendicular to the wavefront in an isotropic medium. Plane wavefronts correspond to parallel rays; spherical wavefronts correspond to rays spreading from or converging toward a point.

When ray optics is not enough: interference, diffraction and polarization require the wave model of light. Geometrical optics does not explain every optical phenomenon.
02 / Reflection

Reflection of light

Reflection occurs when light reaches a boundary and returns to the original medium.

Laws of reflection

  1. The incident ray, reflected ray and normal at the point of incidence lie in the same plane.
  2. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.
∠i = ∠r

Angles are measured from the normal, not from the surface.

Types of reflection

Regular reflection: parallel incident rays reflect in one direction from a smooth surface, producing a clear image.

Diffuse reflection: microscopic surface irregularities send rays in many directions. Each small region still obeys the law of reflection.

Ray diagram: plane mirror

Schematic only; the normal is perpendicular to the mirror at the point of incidence.

Plane mirror image

  • Virtual and erect
  • Same size as the object
  • Laterally inverted
  • Image distance = object distance
  • Cannot be caught on a screen
m = hᵢ / hₒ = +1

Multiple reflections

Two or more mirrors can form multiple images. For two plane mirrors inclined at angle θ, the number depends on symmetry and object position.

N = 360°/θ − 1

This common expression applies when 360°/θ is an integer and the object is on the angle bisector. Otherwise use the appropriate image-count rule for the arrangement.

03 / Refraction

Refraction of light

Refraction is the change in direction of light as it crosses between media because its speed changes. Frequency remains unchanged at the boundary.

Refractive index

The absolute refractive index of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in that medium.

n = c/v

Here c ≈ 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and v is the speed in the medium. A larger n means a lower light speed.

Snell's law

For light passing from medium 1 to medium 2:

n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r

Angles i and r are measured from the normal. Relative refractive index: n₂₁ = n₂/n₁ = sin i/sin r.

Refraction at a flat boundary

Entering a higher-index medium, a ray bends towards the normal. Entering a lower-index medium, it bends away from the normal. At normal incidence, it changes speed but not direction.

Optical density

Optical density describes how strongly a medium slows light. It is not the same as mass density. The higher-index medium is optically denser relative to the other medium.

Apparent depth

For near-normal viewing from air into a transparent medium with a plane surface:

n ≈ real depth / apparent depth

The bottom of a pool therefore appears shallower than it really is.

04 / Total internal reflection

Total internal reflection (TIR)

When light travels from a higher refractive index medium to a lower one, the refracted ray moves farther from the normal as the incidence angle increases.

Two conditions

  1. Light must travel from optically denser (higher n) to optically rarer (lower n) medium.
  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle.

Critical angle

The angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the refracted ray travels along the boundary (r = 90°).

sin C = n₂/n₁ (n₁ > n₂)

For a medium to air: sin C = 1/n.

Three cases

i < C

Refraction occurs; part of the light may also reflect.

i = C

The refracted ray grazes along the interface.

i > C

Total internal reflection occurs; no propagating refracted ray enters the second medium.

Applications

  • Optical fibres: light is guided along a core by repeated total internal reflection. Used in communications and medical endoscopes.
  • Prisms: can turn light through large angles with high reflectivity.
  • Brilliance of diamonds: suitable internal geometry and refractive index produce repeated internal reflections.
  • Mirage: gradual refractive-index changes in air can bend rays and produce apparent displaced images; it is not simply reflection from a literal water surface.
05 / Spherical mirrors

Concave and convex mirrors

A spherical mirror is a reflecting surface that forms part of a sphere. Its principal axis passes through its pole and centre of curvature.

Key terms

  • Pole (P): centre of the mirror surface.
  • Centre of curvature (C): centre of the parent sphere.
  • Radius (R): distance PC.
  • Principal focus (F): point where paraxial parallel rays converge or appear to diverge.
  • Focal length (f): distance PF.
f = R/2

Mirror equation and magnification

1/f = 1/v + 1/um = hᵢ/hₒ = −v/u

Use one consistent Cartesian sign convention. For the real-is-positive convention often used in school physics, signs may be written differently; never mix conventions in one calculation.

Principal rays for a concave mirror

For a concave mirror: a ray parallel to the principal axis reflects through F; a ray through F reflects parallel to the axis; a ray through C retraces its path.

Convex mirror

It always forms a virtual, erect and diminished image for a real object. Its wide field of view makes it useful for vehicle rear-view and security mirrors.

Uses of concave mirrors

Shaving/makeup mirrors, dentist mirrors, reflecting telescopes and reflectors in lamps or searchlights. The image depends on object distance.

06 / Thin lenses

Converging and diverging lenses

A lens refracts light at two surfaces. A thin lens is one whose thickness is small compared with its radii of curvature and relevant object/image distances.

Convex lens

Usually thicker at the centre than at the edges. In air, it is converging for paraxial parallel rays and has positive focal length.

Concave lens

Usually thinner at the centre than at the edges. In air, it is diverging for paraxial parallel rays and has negative focal length.

Lens equation

1/f = 1/v − 1/u

For the Cartesian sign convention: distances are measured from the optical centre; distances in the direction of incident light are positive and those opposite are negative.

Magnification and power

m = hᵢ/hₒ = v/uP = 1/f (f in metres)

Power is measured in dioptres (D). A converging lens has positive power; a diverging lens has negative power in air.

Lens maker's formula

1/f = (nₗ/nₘ − 1)(1/R₁ − 1/R₂)

For a thin lens in a surrounding medium: nₗ is the lens refractive index, nₘ the surrounding-medium index, and R₁ and R₂ are signed radii of curvature under the chosen Cartesian convention. In air, nₘ ≈ 1.

The sign of each radius depends on the direction of curvature and the convention. State the convention before substituting values.

07 / Prisms and dispersion

Prisms, deviation and colour

A prism is a transparent refracting medium bounded by two plane surfaces inclined at an angle called the prism angle A.

Angle of deviation

The angle between the direction of the incident ray (produced forward) and the emergent ray.

δ = i + e − A

i = angle of incidence, e = angle of emergence, A = prism angle.

Minimum deviation

At minimum deviation, the path is symmetric: i = e and r₁ = r₂ = A/2.

n = sin[(A + δₘ)/2] / sin(A/2)

Dispersion of white light

Dispersion is the splitting of white light into its component colours because refractive index depends on wavelength. In ordinary glass, violet light generally deviates more than red light.

Scattering vs dispersion

Dispersion separates wavelengths through wavelength-dependent refraction. Scattering redirects light through interactions with particles or fluctuations in a medium. They are related to colour effects but are different processes.

Rainbow

A primary rainbow forms through refraction, dispersion and one internal reflection in water droplets. Red is on the outer edge and violet on the inner edge of the primary bow.

08 / Optical instruments

How lenses build useful images

Optical instruments use mirrors and lenses to control light and improve how objects are viewed or recorded.

Simple microscope / magnifying glass

A single converging lens with the object inside its focal length produces a virtual, erect, magnified image.

M ≈ D/f (final image at infinity)M ≈ 1 + D/f (near point)

D is the least distance of distinct vision, commonly taken as 25 cm for a standard eye.

Compound microscope

Uses an objective lens of short focal length to make a real, enlarged intermediate image, then an eyepiece to magnify it.

Approximate magnifying power for normal adjustment:

M ≈ (L/fₒ)(D/fₑ)

L is tube length; fₒ and fₑ are objective and eyepiece focal lengths.

Astronomical telescope

The objective forms a real, inverted image of a distant object near its focal plane. The eyepiece magnifies that image.

|M| = fₒ/fₑTube length ≈ fₒ + fₑ

For normal adjustment, the final image is at infinity. The angular magnification is negative if image inversion is included in the sign.

Camera

A converging lens forms a real, inverted image on a sensor or film. Focusing changes lens-to-sensor geometry; aperture controls admitted light and affects depth of field.

Human eye

The cornea and crystalline lens focus light on the retina. The iris controls pupil size. Accommodation changes lens curvature to focus at different distances.

Common vision corrections

Myopia (short-sightedness): distant images focus in front of the retina; corrected with a diverging lens.

Hyperopia (long-sightedness): near images tend to focus behind the retina; corrected with a converging lens.

Presbyopia: age-related reduction in accommodation; may be corrected with suitable reading, bifocal or progressive lenses.

Understand the calculations

Example 1 • Refraction

Find the refracted angle

Light travels from air (n₁ = 1.00) into glass (n₂ = 1.50) at i = 30°.

1.00 sin 30° = 1.50 sin rsin r = 0.5/1.5 = 0.333r ≈ 19.5°

The ray bends towards the normal.

Example 2 • Lens power

Find the power

A converging lens has focal length 20 cm = 0.20 m.

P = 1/f = 1/0.20 = +5 D

The positive sign indicates a converging lens in air.

Glass to air

For glass with n = 1.50 into air:

sin C = 1/1.50 = 0.667C ≈ 41.8°

Incidence above this angle produces total internal reflection.

11 / Image quality

Aberrations and limitations

Spherical aberration

Rays far from the principal axis may focus at a different point from paraxial rays. It can be reduced with suitable lens shapes, stops or combinations of elements.

Chromatic aberration

Different wavelengths refract by different amounts and may focus at different distances. Achromatic lens combinations reduce this effect.

Coma, astigmatism and distortion

Off-axis points can form comet-like images (coma), different tangential and sagittal focus positions (astigmatism), or geometrically distorted images (distortion).

Paraxial approximation

Many simple mirror and lens equations assume rays make small angles with the principal axis and stay close to it. Real optical systems can depart from these ideal assumptions.

12 / Key vocabulary

Glossary

Use these concise definitions when revising.

Core terms

Incident ray: ray approaching a surface.

Reflected ray: ray returning from a surface.

Refracted ray: ray transmitted into another medium.

Normal: perpendicular to a surface at the point of incidence.

Real image: formed where rays actually meet; can be projected on a screen.

Virtual image: formed where rays appear to come from; cannot be projected on a screen.

Lens and mirror terms

Optical centre: reference point near the centre of a thin lens through which a paraxial ray passes approximately undeviated.

Principal axis: reference line through the centres/poles of an optical system.

Focal plane: plane through the principal focus perpendicular to the principal axis.

Magnification: ratio of image size to object size.

Power: reciprocal of focal length in metres.

13 / Check your understanding

Frequently asked questions

Does light slow down in glass?

Yes. Its speed is lower in glass than in vacuum. Its frequency remains the same at a stationary boundary, while its wavelength changes.

Can a virtual image be photographed?

A camera lens can form a real image of the light rays arriving from the apparent source location. The virtual image itself is not a physical meeting of rays, but optical systems can record the corresponding light field.

Why do convex mirrors give a wide field of view?

They diverge reflected rays, allowing a larger region of the surroundings to be seen in a smaller mirror. The trade-off is a diminished image.

Are all convex lenses converging?

Not in every surrounding medium. A lens's behaviour depends on its refractive index relative to the surrounding medium. The usual school-level statement assumes the lens is in air.

What is the difference between ray optics and wave optics?

Ray optics tracks paths and image formation. Wave optics accounts for effects such as interference, diffraction and polarization that require wave properties.
